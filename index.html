About the study

This project explores how young people in Morocco perceive and experience gender equality. It asks about harassment and discrimination, social and beauty pressures, family expectations and differential treatment, safety and mobility, gender roles, and access to education about relationships, consent and body autonomy.

The survey was anonymous and distributed through social media. Respondents could answer in English, French or Arabic, and the three language versions were merged into a single analysis-ready dataset for this website.

Because respondents were reached through social media and chose to take part, the sample is self-selected and skews toward certain regions and school types. The results are best read as a rich, exploratory snapshot — a starting point for conversation, not a representative measurement of a whole generation.

The research question, survey design and data collection are the author's own. The data analysis and this website were built with AI assistance, working only from the real responses.

    Methodology & limitations

    How the data was handled

    Three exports — English, French and Arabic — were parsed and merged into one dataset. Equivalent questions and answer options were matched across languages and standardized to common English labels (for example, Femme, أنثى and Female all map to “Female”). The 1–10 equality rating was kept numeric.

    “Prefer not to say”, “Not applicable” and blank answers are kept separate and excluded from the relevant percentages rather than being counted as No. Charts state the sample size (n) they are based on.

    Data-quality notes

    • No exact duplicate response patterns were detected.
    • No contact information, names or usernames were present in the analyzed exports; nothing of that kind is stored or shown.
    • The optional “women earning more than men” gender-roles question was not present in the response exports, so it is not charted. Gender-role attitudes are explored through family treatment and the open-ended themes instead.
    • Age bands follow the actual survey responses (14–17, 18–20, 20–25).

    What these numbers can and can't say

    • Exploratory, not representative. Respondents were recruited through social media and chose to take part. They are not a random or representative sample of Moroccan youth.
    • Skewed sample. The sample leans female, aged 14–17, in private schools, and concentrated in a few regions. Read every figure as “among these respondents”.
    • Small subgroups. Several regions and categories have very few respondents. The interactive explorer flags small samples and avoids strong claims about tiny groups.
    • Association, not causation. Where two things move together, that is a link — not proof that one causes the other.
    • Self-report. Answers reflect what people were willing to share in an anonymous survey.

    In short: this is a careful, transparent look at what a group of young Moroccans said — an invitation to look closer, not a final word.
